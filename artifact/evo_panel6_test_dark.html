Step 9 · in motion

Watch the map assemble itself

The map above is the finish line. This is the race. The model starts with 192 random numbers per champion. Then it reads the drafts of 5,043 real pro games, and after every wrong guess it nudges every number a tiny step. Nobody ever tells it what a role is.

To film the race, we retrained one copy of the model on the same data and saved every number 56 times along the way. The story below has four beats: one champion, then why two champions end up alike, then all 168 at once, then the champions that break the pattern.

How role clusters form, in four beats

Step through with the buttons. Every number shown is real, from the saved snapshots and the draft record.

The model has exactly one job. Before a match, the two teams take turns choosing characters. That is the draft, and the characters are called champions. There are 168 of them. Show the model the choices made so far, and it guesses which champion gets taken next. That is the only thing it was ever trained to do.

To do that job, it keeps 192 numbers per champion. Here is Rakan, one of the 168. To the model he is nothing but those numbers — no image, no lore, not even a name. We will not tell you what any single number means. Nobody knows, and you do not need to know. Watch the shape of the whole list instead. Each of the 192 numbers is one bar, above or below the centre line:

The list starts as random noise. Training nudges it millions of times, and it stops looking random. That shape is the model’s entire idea of Rakan. So the next question is why any other champion would end up with a similar one.

Show me the actual digits

The first 12 of Rakan’s 192 numbers, in thousandths. The column positions carry no meaning. They are just the order the numbers happen to sit in.

Champions that compete for the same job end up with the same numbers. Think of building a team. A pro team fields exactly five champions, one for each role. So if you have already chosen Rakan, you will never also choose Alistar. They want the same seat — rivals for one slot, not partners. Here is that pattern in 10,294 real team line-ups.

Rakan and Alistar are alternatives to each other, so they never appear together. Rakan and Orianna do different jobs, so they turn up together about as often as pure chance would predict. The model is only ever asked one question: who gets picked next? Rakan and Alistar keep being the answer in the same spots, and every nudge that pushes one of them pushes the other the same way.

So their lists of 192 numbers drift toward the same shape. To make that easy to see, all three rows below are sorted in one shared order — by Rakan’s values, smallest to largest. The order itself means nothing. It is only a way to line the rows up. The dark line on each row is a running average, so you are comparing shapes rather than individual bars.

Rakan’s line climbs by construction, because the sort put his numbers in order. The question is what the other two do under that same order. Alistar’s line climbs with it. Orianna’s stays flat — her numbers have nothing to do with Rakan’s.

Measured properly, that resemblance is one number per pair. It runs from −1 (opposite shapes) through 0 (nothing in common) to +1 (identical), and it uses all 192 numbers at once. Here it is across the whole of training:

Nobody ever told the model that Rakan and Alistar play the same role. It only read drafts. The same pull acted on every pair of champions at the same time — that is the next beat.

Most champions end up next to others who play their role — 0.704 of the time, against 0.20 by chance. Each dot is one champion, coloured by its true role, a label the model never saw. The score under the map is that number: the share of a champion’s five nearest neighbours that play its role. Chance is 0.20 because there are five roles, so one neighbour in five would match on luck alone. Most is doing real work in that sentence. Parts of this map are tidy and parts are a mixed jumble. The champions in the wrong place explain the most. The finished map is not five tidy clusters. That is not sloppiness in the model. A dot lands away from its own kind for exactly two reasons, and we can measure both.

Reason one: the model hardly ever saw it. Sort all 168 champions by how often they appear in the draft record, then measure how well each quarter sorted itself by role. The dotted line marks 0.20 — what you would get from pure chance:

Champions pros rarely draft give the model almost nothing to learn from, so their numbers stay close to the random noise they started as. Evidence is the raw material here: no drafts, no pattern.

Reason two: it genuinely does two jobs. That dip in the last quarter is not noise. Some champions get played in more than one role, so there is no single right place to put them:

Corki is drafted about as often as Rakan, and the model still cannot pin him down. Pros play him in more than one role. That is not the model failing. That is the model correctly reporting that the question has no single answer.

So the jumble in beat 3 is not a mess to look past. It is the map naming the champions the pros themselves refuse to file under one role — and the ones they almost never draft. Read the tidy parts to see what the model learned. Read the messy parts to see what the drafts never told it.
